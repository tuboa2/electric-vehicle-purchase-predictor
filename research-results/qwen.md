## Hierarchical Stacking Architecture: Mitigating Collinearity and Boundary Miscalibration

The primary objective of bridging the 0.00038 ROC-AUC gap to achieve Kaggle Rank 1 in Playground Series Season 6 Episode 9 necessitates an ensemble architecture that transcends conventional wisdom and directly confronts the unique statistical properties of the synthetic dataset . The evidence overwhelmingly indicates that a flat fusion ensemble, where multiple models are blended in a single optimization step, is fundamentally flawed for this specific challenge due to two critical issues: extreme model collinearity within the global backbone and severe miscalibration from a boundary specialist model when applied globally . The proposed solution is a meticulously designed two-tier hierarchical stacking architecture. This structure first establishes a robust global prediction through a diverse set of models operating in logit space, and then applies a localized, gated refinement exclusively to the region of highest uncertainty. This approach is not merely an alternative but a mathematically necessary framework to preserve error diversity, maintain model calibration, and extract the final marginal gains required for victory. The success of this strategy hinges on its ability to navigate the inherent structure of the problem, specifically the discrete cohort-based data generation process and the sharply defined decision boundaries it creates .

The failure of a flat fusion architecture stems directly from the high degree of correlation among the foundational Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT) models intended for the global backbone. The diagnostic report reveals that the Free-Tree and Base-Margin variants of LGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost share a correlation exceeding 0.998 . When subjected to a linear optimizer such as Nelder-Mead or Non-Negative Least Squares (NNLS), which are common in flat blending approaches, such a high level of collinearity causes the optimization algorithm to become unstable [[127](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e5/writeups/5th-place-solution-a-99-model-logit-stack), [130](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e4/writeups/error-diversity-matters-200-model-stacking-soluti)]. The optimizer will inevitably assign near-zero weights to all but one of the highly correlated models, collapsing the ensemble onto a single "best" tree variant while discarding the orthogonal predictive power of the others . This action nullifies the very purpose of including multiple models, which is to leverage their diverse error patterns—a principle known as error diversity [[130](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e4/writeups/error-diversity-matters-200-model-stacking-soluti)]. A winning ensemble relies on this diversity to improve overall performance, and a flat fusion technique would destroy it at the outset by over-relying on a single source of information [[137](https://www.kaggle.com/code/tamerlanomralinov/s6e9-cv-0-946072-lb-0-94614)]. Furthermore, the inclusion of a specialized model trained solely on a narrow band of uncertainty introduces another layer of complexity that a flat blend cannot handle. The boundary specialist model, trained on samples where the `Buy Score` is close to the decision threshold (`|Buy Score - 5.5| < 0.5`), possesses no meaningful calibration outside this specific zone . If its predictions were blended globally with the backbone models, it would inject significant miscalibration into the tails of the probability distribution, where the backbone models are already performing well. This would degrade the overall quality of the predictions and negatively impact the ROC-AUC score, which rewards models that rank positive instances higher than negative ones across all possible thresholds [[26](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Receiver_operating_characteristic), [287](https://blog.trainindata.com/auc-roc-analysis/)].

To overcome these fundamental limitations, the proposed two-tier hierarchical stacking architecture provides a structurally sound and mathematically rigorous solution. This design separates the global modeling task from the local refinement task, ensuring each component operates in its optimal domain. The architecture consists of two distinct tiers: a Global Backbone in Tier 1 and a Boundary-Gated Refinement in Tier 2.

Tier 1, the Global Backbone, is constructed as a convex combination of three diverse prediction streams operating in logit space. This tier is responsible for generating a stable, well-calibrated baseline prediction across the entire feature space. The three streams are:
1.  **Stream A: Free-Tree GBDTs:** This stream consists of four different GBDT models—LightGBM, XGBoost, and two CatBoost instances—configured with free-tree structures. These models form the largest component of the backbone, contributing 45% of the final logit. Their role is to capture the general, global patterns in the data without being constrained by any specific margin initialization.
2.  **Stream B: Base-Margin GBDTs:** This stream also comprises four models (LGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost ×2), but they are configured with base-margin initialization. This technique involves setting a custom starting point for the boosting process, often informed by prior knowledge or preliminary analysis [[59](https://medium.com/@joachimiak.krzysztof/the-forgotten-initial-estimator-in-gbms-65d6c8354572), [61](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials/intercept.html)]. By initializing the model with a base margin, the algorithm can start closer to the true underlying function, potentially leading to faster convergence and improved performance. This stream contributes another 45% to the backbone, matching the scale of the free-tree stream to reflect their perceived similar importance and complementary nature.
3.  **Stream C: Deep Learning Models:** This stream includes three state-of-the-art deep learning architectures: FT-Transformer, TabNet, and DCN-v2. These models are included to provide a third, highly different source of predictions, capturing complex, non-linear interactions that tree-based models might miss. They contribute 10% to the backbone, acknowledging their potential but limiting their influence to prevent them from overwhelming the more established GBDT streams.

The blending of these three streams is performed in logit space, which is the logarithm of the odds ($log(p/(1-p))$). This transformation is superior to a simple average of probabilities because it operates on the log-odds scale, which is more robust to extreme probabilities and better suited for combining models with different underlying distributions [[86](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)]. The formula for the combined logit prediction ($\hat{L}_{backbone}$) is given by the weighted sum of the individual logits:

$$
\hat{L}_{backbone} = 0.45 \cdot logit(\hat{p}_{Free-Tree}) + 0.45 \cdot logit(\hat{p}_{Base-Margin}) + 0.10 \cdot logit(\hat{p}_{Deep-Learning})
$$

This formulation ensures that the diverse signals from the three streams are integrated in a way that preserves their relative strengths. The use of fixed weights (45%, 45%, 10%) represents an informed prior based on the expected performance and diversity of each stream, avoiding the need for a computationally expensive and potentially unstable optimization step on the already-correlated GBDT components. The resulting logit value is then transformed back into a probability ($\hat{p}_{backbone}$) using the logistic function before being passed to Tier 2.

Tier 2, the Boundary-Gated Refinement, acts as a local expert that fine-tunes the backbone's predictions precisely where they are most uncertain. This tier uses a Gaussian gating function, $g(x)$, to smoothly interpolate between the global backbone prediction and the specialist model's prediction . The gate is activated only when the input sample falls within a specific region of uncertainty around the decision boundary. The formula for the final prediction ($\hat{p}_{final}$) is:

$$
\hat{p}_{final} = (1 - g(x)) \cdot \hat{p}_{backbone} + g(x) \cdot \hat{p}_{specialist}
$$

The Gaussian gate is defined as:

$$
g(x) = \exp\left(-\frac{(Buy\_Score - 5.61235)^2}{2 \times 0.25^2}\right)
$$

This function is centered at a Buy Score of 5.61235, with a standard deviation of 0.25, creating a smooth bell-shaped curve. When the backbone's predicted Buy Score is far from the center (i.e., in the tails, such as below 5.0 or above 6.0), the exponent becomes a large negative number, driving $g(x)$ towards zero. In this case, the final prediction is almost entirely the backbone's prediction. However, as the backbone's prediction approaches the central region of uncertainty, $g(x)$ increases, allowing the specialist model's correction to have a greater effect. The specialist model itself is a highly tuned XGBoost model trained specifically on the subset of data where the absolute difference between the ground-truth score and 5.5 is less than 0.5 . This targeted training allows it to learn the subtle patterns in the ambiguous region where the global backbone may falter. The Gaussian gate guarantees that this specialist intervention is localized, preventing the injection of out-of-distribution noise and preserving the calibration of the backbone's strong predictions in the tails . This architectural principle is analogous to modern deep learning techniques like Gated Additive Tree Ensembles (GATE) and GatedTabTransformer, which have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on tabular data by adaptively routing information through different pathways based on input context [[81](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.08548v4), [84](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/gatedtabtransformer), [131](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00199)].

In summary, the two-tier hierarchical stack is the optimal architecture for this competition. It is not simply a preference but a mathematical necessity dictated by the dataset's properties. It successfully mitigates the catastrophic failure mode of collinearity in flat fusion by providing a structured, multi-stream approach. It intelligently integrates a powerful but localized specialist model without corrupting the global prediction. By separating global reasoning from local refinement and using mathematically sound techniques like logit-space blending and Gaussian gating, this architecture provides the most robust and effective path to extracting the final, crucial 0.00038 points needed to claim the top rank.

## Feature Engineering for Cohort-Specific Correction of Simpson's Paradox

A successful machine learning strategy for the Kaggle Playground Series s6e9 must extend beyond sophisticated modeling to encompass deep data-centric insights. The provided diagnostic materials reveal that the synthetic dataset is generated using a procedural logic that partitions the data into distinct cohorts based on the `City_Type` variable, with strict, hard-coded limits on infrastructure variables . Attempting to model this data with generic feature engineering techniques risks introducing noise and obscuring the true underlying relationships. The most effective approach is to engineer features that explicitly encode this latent cohort structure, thereby correcting for the spurious correlations induced by Simpson's Paradox. This strategy aligns perfectly with the data's origin, transforming the model's understanding from a noisy aggregate view to a nuanced, conditional one. By strictly defining features based on the native `City_Type` column and applying within-cohort normalization, the model can accurately capture the differential relationship between charging infrastructure and EV adoption across rural, suburban, and urban environments.

Simpson's Paradox is a statistical phenomenon where a trend appears in several groups of data but disappears or reverses when the groups are combined [[8](https://www.facebook.com/statisticsglobecom/videos/simpsons-paradox-is-a-fascinating-statistical-phenomenon-where-the-relationship-/1134361872195017/), [159](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simpson%27s_paradox)]. This paradox highlights the critical importance of examining how data is structured across different subgroups before drawing conclusions [[1](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/enzo-infantes_simpsons-paradox-i-had-never-come-activity-7306031641951371267-TdLh)]. In the context of the s6e9 dataset, an apparent negative correlation exists between the total number of nearby charging stations (`Total_Charging`) and the likelihood of EV purchase when all data is considered together . This is misleading because the data generator has embedded a confounding variable: `City_Type`. Rural areas, which have a higher baseline adoption rate for EVs (19.34%), inherently have fewer charging stations, while Urban areas, with a lower adoption rate (16.11%), have more stations . Combining these disparate groups creates a spurious negative correlation that masks the true relationship within each group. Correcting for this paradox is essential for building a model that reflects the reality of the data-generating process.

The diagnostic report provides empirical proof of the data's discrete, cohort-based generation mechanics. The data generator uses `City_Type` as a switch to enforce strict hard caps on charging station counts:
*   **Rural:** Charging at home ∈ [0, 3], Charging at work ∈ [0, 8]
*   **Suburban:** Charging at home ∈ [0, 9], Charging at work ∈ [0, 12]
*   **Urban:** Charging at home ∈ [0, 14], Charging at work ∈ [1, 19] 

Crucially, there is no continuous population density variable; the grouping is purely categorical and procedural . Any attempt to create a composite proxy for city type would be a fundamental error, as it would blur these sharp, artificially imposed boundaries and introduce estimation noise. The goal of feature engineering is to make the data more informative for the model [[166](https://www.kaggle.com/code/prashant111/a-reference-guide-to-feature-engineering-methods), [171](https://www.datacamp.com/tutorial/feature-engineering-kaggle)], and the most direct way to do so is to respect the data's intrinsic structure. Therefore, all corrective features must be engineered strictly using the native `City_Type` column.

The proposed feature engineering strategy consists of two key components designed to dismantle the spurious correlation and highlight the true cohort-specific dynamics. The first component involves calculating within-cohort Z-score standardizations. This mathematical operation normalizes a feature's value by subtracting the mean of that feature for its specific city type and dividing by its standard deviation, effectively re-centering and re-scaling the data within each group. The formulas for these features are as follows:

*   `feat_charging_home_z_city`: $\frac{Charging\_Stations\_Near\_Home - \mu_{Charging\_Stations\_Near\_Home, City\_Type}}{\sigma_{Charging\_Stations\_Near\_Home, City\_Type}}$
*   `feat_charging_work_z_city`: $\frac{Charging\_Stations\_Near\_Work - \mu_{Charging\_Stations\_Near\_Work, City\_Type}}{\sigma_{Charging\_Stations\_Near\_Work, City\_Type}}$
*   `feat_total_charging_z_city`: $\frac{total\_charging - \mu_{total\_charging, City\_Type}}{\sigma_{total\_charging, City\_Type}}$

Where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ represent the mean and standard deviation, respectively, calculated over all samples belonging to the same `City_Type` . This normalization transforms the raw count data into a standardized metric of "unusualness" within each cohort. For example, a person in a Rural area with 3 home charging stations becomes a very high-value outlier (+2 or +3 standard deviations), whereas a person in an Urban area with the same number of stations is a low-value observation (-2 or -3 standard deviations). When these Z-scores are calculated, the previously observed negative correlation between `Total_Charging` and EV adoption flips sign, becoming a positive correlation (+0.0145) . This demonstrates that the raw relationship was indeed misleading, and that expressing the feature relative to its own cohort's distribution is the correct procedure. This technique is akin to z-score normalization used to ensure equal weighting in distance-based sampling and is a standard practice for handling heterogeneous data [[25](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7080/14/1/54), [30](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/numerical-data/normalization)].

The second component of the feature engineering strategy involves creating hard saturation indicators. These binary features signal when a sample has reached the maximum capacity for infrastructure as defined by its city type. These flags directly encode the discrete limits imposed by the data generator and provide unambiguous information about the upper bound of available resources. The formulas are straightforward logical checks:

*   `feat_rural_home_saturated`: $(City\_Type == 'Rural') \land (Charging\_Stations\_Near\_Home >= 3)$
*   `feat_urban_work_saturated`: $(City\_Type == 'Urban') \land (Charging\_Stations\_Near\_Work >= 18)$

These features are cast as `int8` to conserve memory . They inform the model that a rural individual with the maximum allowed home charging stations represents a fundamentally different scenario from an urban individual with the same number of stations. This is a classic application of feature engineering that leverages domain knowledge to create more discriminative features [[281](https://medium.com/swlh/my-journey-to-kaggle-competitions-grandmaster-status-a-look-into-feature-engineering-titanic-99a0db7f25dd), [316](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ft77eXtn30Q)]. These indicators act as powerful signals, helping the model understand the constraints under which individuals in different city types operate.

By implementing these two sets of features, the model gains explicit access to the cohort-specific structure that drives the outcome. It moves from a position of seeing a noisy, aggregated relationship to one of understanding the conditional relationships within each distinct group. This is a direct and powerful application of the principle that segmenting data is crucial for accurate business and scientific analysis to avoid misleading insights [[4](https://mixpanel.com/blog/avoiding-data-fallacies-and-biases-simpsons-paradox-and-the-importance-of-segmenting-data/), [9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10806368/)]. Given that the ground-truth data is explicitly structured around these discrete cohorts, these engineered features are not just beneficial—they are essential for closing the gap between the model's learned representation and the reality of the data's synthetic origin. They transform the feature set from a collection of raw numerical values into a rich description of the underlying system, providing the final piece of the puzzle needed to surpass the current leaderboard leader.

## Class-Balanced Pseudo-Labeling to Preserve Model Calibration

The final step in achieving the target ROC-AUC score involves leveraging the vast unlabeled test set to augment the training data, a technique known as pseudo-labeling [[31](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s3e3/writeups/kaggleqrdl-using-pseudo-labels-to-predict-fitness-), [184](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10449823/)]. However, the method of selecting and incorporating these pseudo-labels is critical. Naive approaches, such as applying fixed probability thresholds, can catastrophically degrade model performance by introducing severe class imbalance and miscalibration [[117](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3757110.3757161)]. The diagnostic analysis reveals that a simple cutoff at probabilities greater than 0.990 or less than 0.008 results in an extremely skewed pseudo-labeled set with a true positive rate of only 0.7%, compared to the ground-truth rate of 17.48% . Training on such a biased dataset forces the model to learn a distorted class distribution, which undermines its ability to produce well-calibrated probabilities and ultimately harms the ROC-AUC score. The correct approach is a class-balanced pseudo-labeling strategy that preserves the true prior distribution of the positive class. This method ensures that the model remains calibrated and that the added information from the unlabeled data genuinely improves its generalization capabilities.

The core flaw in naive thresholding lies in its disregard for the underlying class distribution. When a model makes predictions on the test set, the distribution of its output probabilities may not match the distribution of the training set. Using fixed thresholds like `p > 0.990` or `p < 0.008` selects a tiny fraction of high-confidence positives and a massive number of high-confidence negatives . The resulting pseudo-labeled dataset becomes heavily dominated by the negative class. For instance, adding 118,000 negative pseudo-labels and only 837 positive ones to the training set creates an extreme 140:1 negative-to-positive ratio . A model trained on this augmented set will inevitably become biased towards predicting the majority class (negative), causing its calibration to collapse and its ability to distinguish between classes to deteriorate. While ROC-AUC is theoretically insensitive to class distribution [[238](https://www.instagram.com/p/DdTcys1Fzoz/), [380](https://www.quora.com/Why-is-AUC-Area-under-ROC-insensitive-to-class-distribution-changes)], poor calibration often indicates a model that has failed to capture the true underlying relationships in the data, which can lead to worse performance on other metrics and less reliable predictions in practice [[86](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html), [164](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.14540)].

An even more dangerous alternative is to use dynamic per-fold thresholds adjusted based on local fold ROC-AUC stability . While this might seem like a way to adapt to the specific characteristics of each fold, it introduces a new problem: fold-to-fold class-prior shift. Different cross-validation folds may contain vastly different numbers of samples from the minority class. One fold might contain 2,000 positive samples, while another contains only 800 . Adjusting the pseudo-labeling threshold for each fold based on its local statistics means that the base models trained on these folds are exposed to different target distributions. This leads to inconsistencies in their predicted probability baselines, breaking the delicate balance required for a coherent and well-calibrated final ensemble [[69](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html), [70](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11412612/)]. The integrity of the stacked ensemble relies on the base models producing predictions that can be meaningfully combined, and inconsistent priors across folds undermine this principle.

The superior and mathematically sound solution is to use a fixed, class-balanced quantile selection method for pseudo-labeling. This approach guarantees that the class distribution of the pseudo-labeled set mirrors the true ground-truth distribution, thereby preserving the model's calibration. The process involves fixing the sample counts rather than the probability thresholds, ensuring that every fold is trained on the exact same proportion of positive and negative examples. The steps are as follows:
1.  **Select Confident Positive Samples:** Identify a fixed number of the most confident positive predictions from the initial model's output. For example, select the top K=5,000 samples with the highest predicted probabilities (e.g., above a reasonable confidence floor like 0.88).
2.  **Select Confident Negative Samples:** Calculate the corresponding number of confident negative samples needed to maintain the ground-truth positive rate of 17.48%. The formula for the number of negative samples (M) is derived from the desired positive rate: $\frac{K}{K+M} = 0.1748$. Solving for M gives $M = K \times \frac{1 - 0.1748}{0.1748}$. For K=5,000, this yields approximately M ≈ 23,600 samples. Select the bottom 23,600 samples with the lowest predicted probabilities (e.g., below a very low threshold like 0.0003).
3.  **Create a Fixed Pseudo-Dataset:** Combine these K positive and M negative samples into a single, augmented training set. This set will contain approximately 28,600 samples, representing about 10% of the test set, with a positive rate of exactly 17.5% .
4.  **Apply Across All Folds:** Crucially, this exact same pseudo-dataset is used for training the base models in every single fold of the cross-validation process . This invariance ensures that every fold model shares the same target prior, preventing fold-to-fold calibration drift and maintaining the stability of the final ensemble.

This class-balanced strategy offers several key advantages. First, it directly addresses the issue of calibration preservation. By maintaining the ground-truth class prior, the model's learned probabilities remain well-calibrated, which is a cornerstone of reliable and high-performing classification systems [[87](https://blog.trainindata.com/probability-calibration-in-machine-learning/), [89](https://www.tidymodels.org/learn/models/calibration/), [91](https://www.machinelearningmastery.com/probability-calibration-for-imbalanced-classification/)]. Second, it ensures stability and reproducibility. Because the pseudo-dataset is fixed, the results are not subject to the random fluctuations that can occur in different folds or with varying confidence thresholds [[72](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.658.pdf)]. Third, it is a robust semi-supervised learning technique. It effectively leverages the unlabeled data to improve model generalization without the risk of "teaching the model wrong" by exposing it to a biased version of reality [[183](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.14013v3), [186](https://medium.com/ai-enthusiast/pseudo-labeling-how-to-maximize-small-datasets-for-big-results-9716ac9a8eb7)]. This method is consistent with advanced literature in semi-supervised learning that emphasizes the importance of re-balancing labels during pseudo-labeling to handle imbalanced datasets effectively [[118](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12559-026-10643-1), [416](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024arXiv240705370L/abstract), [417](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/papers/Oh_DASO_Distribution-Aware_Semantics-Oriented_Pseudo-Label_for_Imbalanced_Semi-Supervised_Learning_CVPR_2022_paper.pdf)].

Finally, to integrate the pseudo-labeled data without overwhelming the original labeled data, a moderate sample weight should be applied. Assigning a weight `w=0.60` to all rows in the augmented dataset is recommended . This prevents the pseudo-labels from having too much influence on the model's gradient updates, treating them as high-confidence but still somewhat uncertain information. This careful balancing act—selecting a sufficient number of high-quality pseudo-labels while moderating their influence—provides a stable and effective way to boost model performance. When combined with stratified k-fold cross-validation, which ensures that each fold maintains the same class distribution as the original dataset, this approach forms a complete and robust pipeline for leveraging the unlabeled test data to push the ROC-AUC score over the 0.94672 threshold [[323](https://medium.com/@pacosun/stratified-k-fold-cross-validation-when-balance-matters-c28b9a7cb9bc), [330](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.StratifiedKFold.html)].
